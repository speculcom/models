How to read a leaderboard: what it measures, then who runs it

Practitioner methodology · key facts carry official sources

A leaderboard is not a model ranking. First ask what it measures, when the items were released, who judges and how the score is computed; then decide whether it matters for your selection.

← Section indexModels home

What is measured: knowledge, reasoning or task completion

MMLU measures multiple-choice knowledge; GSM8K measures grade-school word problems; SWE-bench measures bug fixing in real repositories; Chatbot Arena measures human preference. They are not the same thing and cannot be summed into one “general capability”. Write your use case in one sentence and find the closest benchmark; if none is close, build 50–100 internal cases instead of forcing a public leaderboard.

Have the items been seen?

Public benchmark items end up in training data. Check model release date, training-data statement and whether the leaderboard uses a time split. Continuously refreshed sets such as LiveCodeBench and LiveBench have lower contamination risk, but their item distribution changes. A score without a benchmark version and submission date is marketing, not evidence.

Who judges, and how is the score computed?

LLM judges have length, style and position bias; human votes have user-distribution bias. Check whether the leaderboard reports judge models, prompt templates, confidence intervals and sample size. A 60% win rate does not mean 60% more capability. Ask who the opponents were, what the items were and how wide the interval is; a few points on a small sample can be noise.

Can it be reproduced, and at what cost?

A reproducible leaderboard publishes code, prompts, versions and runtime. If it cannot be reproduced, treat it as reference only. Also count the cost: SWE-bench needs containers and tests; arena-style evaluations need many human votes. A benchmark that costs more than the decision is not worth running.
